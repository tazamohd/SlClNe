01 — Nobody keeps the plan in their head

The board

Nine bays on one screen. The schedule proposes: it knows which technician is certified for the job, which parts landed this morning, and what the customer was told about collection time. A human still approves every move — and every move it makes writes an audit row, so at five o’clock you can ask why a car went to bay 6 and get an answer instead of a shrug.

02 — The car speaks first

What breaks next

Fault codes tell you what already broke. By 2030 the interesting question is the other one. The car’s own diagnostic port, plus eleven years of what went wrong on the same model in the same heat, produces a ranked list of what fails next and roughly when — each line carrying the confidence it earned, so an advisor can say “not yet” out loud and mean it.

WHAT EXISTS TODAY: an OBD integration that reads the port, a multi-point inspection with severity per item, and the full service history behind both. What 2030 adds is the model on top — and a model is a data problem and a year of records, not an invention.

03 — Some jobs do not need a workshop

The network

Eleven branches and nine service vans across the kingdom, on one schedule. A battery check, a brake pad, a diagnostic read — a van does those in a car park while the customer is at work, and books the job against the same vehicle record the branch would have used. Anything needing a lift still comes in. That is not a limitation; it is an honest division of labour.
